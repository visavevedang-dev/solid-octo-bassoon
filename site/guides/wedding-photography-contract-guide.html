Wedding Photography Contract Guide — What to Include

A wedding photography contract does one job: it makes sure both sides agree, in writing, on what happens if the day doesn't go exactly to plan. Most disputes between photographers and clients aren't about the photos — they're about expectations nobody wrote down.

The non-negotiables

Whatever format you use, a wedding photography contract should cover:

  • Date, time, and location(s) — including any second venue for the reception.
  • Coverage hours — when you arrive and when you're contracted to leave.
  • Deliverables — how many edited photos, in what format, delivered by when.
  • Payment schedule — deposit amount, balance due date, and what happens if payment is late.
  • Cancellation and rescheduling terms — for both sides, not just the client.
  • Copyright and usage rights — who can use the photos, and where.
  • A backup/second-shooter clause — what happens if you're unable to shoot due to illness or emergency.

One agreement doesn't fit every booking

Not every booking needs the same level of detail. A short engagement session and a full wedding day carry very different risk, so it's reasonable to use different agreements for each:

  • A simple agreement for smaller or lower-risk bookings — covers the essentials (date, deliverables, payment) without the full legal detail of a wedding-day contract.
  • A full wedding contract for the main event — the one that needs every clause above, since the stakes (and the date's non-repeatability) are highest.
  • An engagement session agreement — shorter than a full wedding contract, since it's a lower-risk, shorter shoot, but still worth having in writing.

Send it early, track it properly

The contract is most useful before the deposit, not after. Sending it as a template rather than drafting from scratch each time also means fewer accidental omissions — the same clauses go out every time, and you can see at a glance which bookings are still waiting on a signature versus which are confirmed.

What a contract can't fix

A contract protects you if something goes wrong — it doesn't replace clear communication beforehand. Walk clients through coverage hours, delivery timelines, and what's and isn't included before they sign, not after. The contract is the record of that conversation, not a substitute for having it.
